Reading the spectra of stars

Starlight carries a hidden message. When it passes through the gas in a star’s atmosphere, some colours are absorbed, leaving dark lines. Each element absorbs at its own wavelengths, like a barcode. In this lab you will learn the barcodes, then use them to find out what stars are made of and how hot they are. It is also good practice for reading spectra before the redshift lab.

1. Every element has its own barcode

Choose one or more elements. The top strip is white light (a continuous spectrum). The middle strip is the element glowing when it is hot, and the bottom strip is the same element absorbing light from a hotter source behind it. Notice that the dark lines are in exactly the same places as the bright ones.

2. Compare star spectra

Pick a star. Move your pointer (or finger) across the spectra: a white guide line shows the wavelength in nm and highlights matching lines in the element strips underneath. The wavelength is in nanometres (nm); violet is about 400 nm and red about 700 nm.

Move over the spectrum to read a wavelength.

4. Where do the elements come from?

The Big BangAlmost all the hydrogen and helium in the universe was made in the first few minutes, about 13.8 billion years ago. The very first stars were made of almost nothing else.
Stars are element factoriesIn the core, fusion turns hydrogen into helium. In massive stars, fusion builds heavier elements such as carbon, oxygen and magnesium, up to iron, where the process stops releasing energy.
Supernovae spread themElements heavier than iron are made in exploding stars and in colliding neutron stars. The explosions scatter everything into space, where new stars form. The iron and calcium in the Sun’s spectrum were made by earlier stars.

Why do the star classes look so different? Most stars are mostly hydrogen. The class (O, B, A, F, G, K, M) depends mainly on the star’s temperature, which controls which atoms can absorb light. Very hot stars strip electrons off atoms, and very cool stars have too little energy to excite hydrogen, so its lines are weak.
